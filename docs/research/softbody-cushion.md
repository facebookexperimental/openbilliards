# softbody-cushion

## KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Real soft body is not worth doing as gameplay physics: the pooltool author explicitly judges realistic soft-body cushion 'unfeasible', not even a single FEM paper on billiard cushions can be found in academia, and all high-fidelity simulators use rigid-body impulse/parameterized models.
- Mathavan 2010 (primary paper verified in full text) gives directly usable measured parameters: energetic COR e=0.98, ball-cushion friction μw=0.14, ball-cloth friction μs≈0.212, and the rigid-body assumption holds only for normal velocity ≤2.5 m/s—this is exactly the quantitative boundary of speed-dependent rebound.
- The ball-cushion contact duration has no public measured value (unconfirmed), but slow-motion evidence and impulse estimates consistently point to the order of 5–15ms and 5–10mm compression—far longer than the 0.25ms of ball-ball, meaning 0.2–0.5ms substeps suffice to cover it; compute is not the bottleneck for real soft body, the missing K-66 material calibration data is.
- K-66 nose height is about 64% of ball diameter (WPA spec 63.5%±1%): higher makes banks longer, lower makes banks shorter and the ball hops; a contact point above the ball center presses the ball toward the bed and the bed then bounces it up, so hop can emerge naturally in a rigid-body model through 'downward outgoing component+ball-slate event', with no soft body needed.
- A parameterized model can run in real time at paper-level accuracy: tailuge/billiards runs the Mathavan numerical model in real time in the browser (even on a Raspberry Pi 4) and reproduces the paper's figures, proving that 'offline grid sweep into a lookup table' is not even a necessary performance compromise.
- A hybrid approach is enough to fool professional players: a commercial simulator (internal implementation unconfirmed, but no sign of soft body) earned praise from professional content creators for its cushion and spin feel—experts judge the outgoing angle/speed/spin mapping, not the solver internals; for visual deformation an impulse-driven vertex shader bulge suffices, cost≈0.
- Phased recommendation: v1 uses corrected Han 2005 + e(v) speed decay + shader deformation; v2 sweeps a 4D grid of the Mathavan/penalty model offline into a lookup table or a small MLP, corrected with Dr. Dave quantitative anchors (sidespin 1/2/3 diamonds) and self-shot 240fps data; v3 real soft body is limited to purely visual local XPBD or an offline FEM data factory.
- If a real-time soft-body visual layer is wanted, the technical route is XPBD + Small Steps (Macklin 2019, original read): n substeps×1 iteration far outperforms 1 large step×n iterations; PhysX 5 FEM soft body is CUDA-only, essentially ruled out for a cross-platform billiards game.
- The cheapest combination of fitting data: digitized Mathavan paper Figs 7–10 + the Dr. Dave HSV video library (key: HSV 6.1 cushion compression, B.41 speed effect) + 240fps phone footage of the incoming/outgoing mapping; only contact-phase detail needs a Chronos-class high-speed camera ($2500–5000).
- Note the parameter convention differences: Mathavan uses sinθ=2/5 (70% of ball diameter, snooker-leaning), Han uses 0.1R–0.2R, American K-66 should actually take sinθ≈0.27—copying the paper constants verbatim gets the rebound angle wrong.

## OPEN QUESTIONS
- Direct measured value of the ball-cushion contact duration: no public measurement found (the papers are all impulse models, Dr. Dave has no number either); the 5–15ms/5–10mm in the report is my own estimate, verifying it with your own high-speed camera tests is recommended.
- The commercial simulator's physics implementation details are entirely undisclosed (whether it uses an in-house engine and its cushion model type are both unconfirmed); only marketing pages and professional player testimonials attest to its feel.
- No public FEM/hyperelasticity paper on billiard cushions found (searched with both English and Chinese keywords); there is no public data on the specific formulation, Shore hardness or Mooney-Rivlin/Ogden/Prony parameters of K-66 rubber, so an offline FEM route requires doing your own material tests.
- The Han 2005 original is a scanned PDF from which no text can be extracted; its specific e/μ values were not verified from the primary source and rely on pooltool's restatement (which also corrected 2 formula errors in the original).
- Dr. Dave's explanation of 'the two mechanisms of speed on the bank angle partially cancelling' (rail throwback vs COR change) is labelled conjecture by its author, with no quantitative validation yet.
- There is no ready-made data for the correct outgoing mapping of the Mathavan model in its failure domain vN>2.5 m/s; it needs self-shot high-speed video or offline FEM to fill in.

## REPORT
# Billiard rail (cushion) soft-body elasticity simulation: feasibility research report

## TL;DR

**Real soft body (runtime FEM/XPBD) is not worth doing as gameplay physics**; academia and the most hardcore simulators (a commercial simulator, pooltool, tailuge/billiards) all take the "rigid-body impulse/parameterized model" route, and professional players already recognize them as "feels real". Recommendation: **runtime parameterized model (start with corrected Han 2005 → Mathavan 2010 offline grid sweep into a lookup table) + shader vertex deformation for the visuals**. Real soft body has value in only two places: (a) generating lookup-table data offline, (b) purely visual local deformation—and for (a), substituting real footage data is often cheaper and more accurate.

---

## 1. Real cushion physics: list of confirmed facts

### 1.1 Material and geometry
- **K-66 profile** (American pool standard): base 1-3/16" (30mm), nose height 1" (25mm), once installed the nose is 1-7/16" above the bed (about 36.5mm, i.e. about 64% of ball diameter for a 2-1/4"/57.15mm ball). The material is vulcanized rubber (gum or synthetic; Wikipedia marks this entry citation-needed, the specific formulation is **unconfirmed**). Carom billiards (carom) uses K-55, with a fabric (canvas) control layer vulcanized into the top to tune the rebound; snooker uses an L shape (e.g. L77). Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiard_table , https://www.legacybilliards.com/blogs/resources/rail-cushion-profiles-which-is-best-for-your-pool-table
- **Nose height standard**: WPA specifies a nose height of 63.5%±1% of ball diameter (62.5%–64.5%). Higher → slower rebound, banks play "long"; lower → faster rebound, banks play "short" and the ball hops more easily (hop). 0.5D hops balls noticeably, 0.7D (center of percussion) presses the ball into the bed and wears a rail groove. Source: https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/table/cushion-nose-height/
- Note the convention differences: the Mathavan 2010 paper takes a contact point height h = 7R/5 (i.e. 70% of ball diameter, sinθ = 2/5), leaning toward the snooker cushion shape; Han 2005 uses ε ≈ 0.1R–0.2R (about 55%–60% of ball diameter). When building a game, θ should be set from the spec you choose (pool: sinθ≈0.27–0.29), rather than copying the paper constants.
- **Temperature/humidity sensitive**: rubber rebound depends strongly on temperature, so international carom rules mandate a heated bed (33–37°C, some up to 45°C), and professional snooker tables are heated too. This by itself shows that a real cushion is a rate+temperature-dependent viscoelastic body. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiard_table , https://surreysnookeracademy.com/why-are-snooker-tables-heated/

### 1.2 Contact duration and compression depth
- **Ball-ball collision** measured at 0.1–0.6ms (Marlow), Sorokin measured 250–300µs. Source: https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/ball/contact-time/
- **Ball-cushion contact duration: no public direct measured value found (unconfirmed)**. But indirect evidence consistently points to **the millisecond range, more than an order of magnitude longer than ball-ball**: the pooltool author, based on slow-motion footage, explicitly writes "rail deforms substantially... the collision lasts far longer than ball-ball" (https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/); Gismeros Moreno 2022 reproduced a 2.724 m/s cushion impact with 2000fps video (the contact spans multiple frames); Dr. Dave has a dedicated HSV 6.1 "Cushion deformation during high-speed bank" slow-motion clip (https://drdavepoolinfo.com/high-speed-video/). Impulse-momentum estimate (m=0.17kg, vₙ=2m/s, half-sine contact): contact duration ~5–15ms, compression depth ~5–10mm, average force of a medium-speed cushion impact ~50–100N—**this is this report's estimate, not a measurement**.
- Key inference: a 5–15ms contact duration means runtime soft body does not need anything as daunting as a "sub-millisecond physics frame" (0.2–0.5ms substeps suffice to cover it); **compute is not the main obstacle for real soft body, calibration data is** (see §3).

### 1.3 Rebound parameters (Mathavan 2010 measured fit, primary source)
The full paper is free: https://drdavepoolinfo.com/physics_articles/Mathavan_IMechE_2010.pdf (Proc. IMechE Part C, 224, 2010, DOI:10.1243/09544062JMES1964). Key points (I extracted and verified them from the full PDF text):
- Measured with a high-speed camera on a Riley Renaissance snooker table, the fit gives **energetic COR eₑ = 0.98, ball-cushion sliding friction μw = 0.14, ball-cloth sliding friction μs ≈ 0.178–0.245 (0.212 taken)**; snooker ball M=0.1406kg, R=26.25mm.
- **The rigid-cushion assumption holds only for a normal incident speed ≤2.5 m/s**: at V₀>2.5m/s the numerical predictions deviate systematically from experiment—this is exactly the quantitative boundary of "the harder you hit, the more the cushion behavior departs from the rigid-body model", and also direct evidence of speed-dependent rebound.
- Numerical method: discretize the impulse into N≈5000 incremental steps of iterative integration (impulse-space ODE), with the compression phase and restitution phase separated, which can compute stick/slip transitions.
- Result figures (Figs 8–10, digitizable as a fitting source): for pure rolling without sidespin, **the rebound angle in the rigid domain depends only on the incidence angle and is independent of speed**; the speed loss is greatest at an incidence angle of about 40°; top spin raises the rebound speed; sidespin significantly changes the rebound angle, and at steep incidence the sidespin direction can even be reversed by the cushion.

### 1.4 Player-perceivable phenomena (Dr. Dave summary, with HSV/NV video numbers)
Source: http://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/bank-kick/effects/
- **The faster the speed, the shorter the bank** (NV 6.6/6.7), with at least two mechanisms: at high speed the ball has no time to regain natural roll (roll lengthens the rebound path); and the "rail throwback" effect of large cushion deformation. But HSV B.41 shows that counterexamples also exist—the angle shortening from deformation and the lengthening effect of COR changing with speed can partially cancel (Dr. Dave's own label: "This is all conjecture").
- **Sidespin**: running spin lengthens the rebound angle and speeds the ball up, reverse spin shortens it and slows it down; on a straight-in, straight-out kick, 1/3, 2/3 and full sidespin cause lateral offsets of about 1, 2 and 3 diamonds respectively (a rare quantitative anchor).
- **Top/bottom spin**: topspin lengthens the kick, draw shortens it; near-rail short-range/power shots shorten the bank because of "less roll".
- **Hop (pop-up/hop)**: contact point above the ball center → the outgoing velocity carries a downward component → the bed's normal force pushes the ball up into a small hop; tables with too low a nose hop balls more noticeably. pooltool explicitly splits it into the two steps "the ball-cushion event produces a downward velocity + a subsequent ball-slate event bounces it up" (https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/).
- **Equipment dependent**: livelier cushions (high normal COR) give shorter banks; Diamond brand tables clearly bank short; new/slick cloth makes banks longer.

## 3. Survey of real-time soft-body techniques and feasibility of "local high-frequency substeps"

- **XPBD + Small Steps** (Macklin et al., SCA'19, https://mmacklin.com/smallsteps.pdf , original read and verified): the core conclusion is that "n substeps × 1 iteration" far outperforms "1 large step × n iterations", greatly reducing constraint error and numerical damping, and it is stable for stiff materials. This fits exactly the "contact lasts only ~10ms" scenario: within a 120Hz render frame, switch to 20–50 substeps (dt≈0.2–0.5ms) only after the ball enters the cushion broad-phase, and run XPBD on a local tet mesh 2–3 ball diameters wide (~1–2k tets)—**the compute is fully under control** (<1ms/frame on the GPU, and the CPU can handle it too).
- **PhysX 5 FEM soft body**: true FEM + dual tet meshes, but **CUDA-only, no CPU fallback**, Poisson ratio<0.5, stiff materials need piled-up iterations (https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.1.1/docs/SoftBodies.html ). Essentially ruled out for a cross-platform billiards game (mobile/web).
- Jolt soft body is PBD vertex soft body (cloth/soft-gel class), Zibra focuses on fluids, and real-time MPM approaches have no advantage for this kind of "thin rubber strip high-speed contact".
- **The real obstacle is not compute, but three things**:
  1. **Material calibration**: there are no public K-66 hyperelastic (Mooney-Rivlin/Ogden) + rate damping (Prony) parameters; doing it yourself means cutting specimen strips for a universal testing machine/DMA. Rubber COR falling with impact speed is a general law (e.g. https://frontiersin.org/journals/mechanical-engineering/articles/10.3389/fmech.2020.00003/full ), and without calibrating the rate term you cannot correctly simulate "hit it hard and it actually plays shorter".
  2. **Result controllability/determinism**: the rebound angle is sensitive to mesh resolution, substep count and iteration order; online matches, replays and AI shot planning all need bit-level or at least highly reproducible trajectories—soft-body solvers are inherently at a disadvantage here.
  3. **Acceptance criterion**: what professional players perceive is whether "the mapping of outgoing angle/speed/spin against the incoming conditions" looks like a real table, not whether the solver internals are soft body. An uncalibrated soft-body simulation will very likely be **more fake** than a calibrated impulse model.
- Conclusion: **"running high-frequency soft-body substeps locally only at the moment of cushion impact" is feasible in engineering terms, but it is recommended only for the visual layer, not for deciding the outgoing trajectory.**

## 4. Hybrid approach evaluation (recommended route)

**Runtime parameterized + visual deformation, can it fool professional players?—The evidence says it can.**
- The makers of this commercial simulator have never disclosed physics implementation details (**internal implementation unconfirmed**); the promotional material is only marketing copy and praise for its feel from professional content creators. There is no sign at all that it runs soft body; the frame-rate budget and online play of products of this kind both point to a parameterized model. In other words: **the product on the market that "best fools experts" is very likely just a parameterized model + good audiovisual feedback**.
- The pooltool author flatly asserts realistic soft-body cushion "unfeasible" and adopts corrected Han 2005; tailuge/billiards in turn proves that the Mathavan numerical model can run directly in real time and reproduce the paper's figures.
- The key to fooling the "eye" is not the deformation itself (contact lasts only ~10ms, the naked eye can barely see a bulge for one or two frames), but: speed-dependent bank shortening, sidespin angle change, hop, the correct speed-loss curve, and secondary cues such as the cushion impact sound/slight cloth movement. For visual deformation a **vertex shader local bulge** (amplitude∝normal impulse, width ~2 ball diameters, 10–20ms half-sine + brief ring-down) suffices, cost≈0, and it adds a lot in slow-motion replays.

## 5. Data sources (for fitting the parameterized model)

1. **The Mathavan 2010 paper itself**: free PDF, Figs 7–10 give rebound speed & angle curves under different speed/topspin/sidespin, which can be digitized and used directly as training/fitting data; the parameters eₑ=0.98, μw=0.14, μs=0.212 are directly usable (snooker equipment; pool needs recalibration).
2. **Dr. Dave resource library**: HSV high-speed video index (https://drdavepoolinfo.com/high-speed-video/ , key: HSV 6.1, 6.2–6.14, A.128–A.141 "shots at the moment of maximum compression", B.20, B.25, B.41) + FAQ qualitative directions (the 1/2/3-diamond sidespin quantitative anchors at https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/bank-kick/effects/ ) + physics literature page (https://drdavepoolinfo.com/physics/ , including the scanned Han 2005 https://drdavepoolinfo.com/physics_articles/Han_paper.pdf ).
3. **Open-source implementations**: tailuge/billiards (GPL-3.0, take care for commercial use, can be rewritten from the papers) ships its own validation tool that "reproduces the Mathavan figures"; pooltool has a derivation worksheet for the corrected Han formulas.
4. **Self-shot high-speed video**: fitting the parameterized model only needs the mapping "incoming (v, angle, spin) → outgoing (v', angle', spin')"; **a 240fps phone + overhead camera position + markers on the ball** are enough to calibrate the incoming/outgoing speed and angle (spin requires marker-point tracking); only seeing contact-phase detail (compression depth, contact duration) requires 1000fps+: Chronos 1.4 about $2500–3500 (1050fps@720p, https://www.dpreview.com/news/8747302245/chronos-1-4-is-a-2500-camera-that-can-shoot-at-21-650-fps ), Chronos 2.1-HD about $5000 (1000fps@1080p). Gismeros Moreno 2022 also used only 2000fps.

## 6. Recommended phased plan

**v1 (1–2 weeks, directly reaching the "high-end simulator" baseline)**
- Event-driven rigid-body framework + **corrected Han 2005** (following pooltool's corrected formulas, mind the two stick/slip branches), θ from the actual K-66 nose height (sinθ≈0.27, not the paper's 2/5).
- Overlay an **e(vₙ) empirical curve** (decaying from vₙ>2.5m/s) to near-empirically reproduce "hit hard, plays shorter"; keep the downward outgoing component → let hop emerge naturally from the ball-slate bounce.
- Visuals: contact event-driven vertex shader bulge + layered sound effects (speed-mapped). This layer alone is enough to support slow-motion replays.

**v2 (enhancement, 2–6 weeks)**
- Offline, run the **Mathavan impulse ODE (or the LN penalty model) to sweep a 4D parameter grid** (vₙ, v_t, sidespin, top/bottom spin), each point outputting 6 outgoing quantities → a lookup table (20⁴≈160,000 grid points, a few MB) or a small 4→6 MLP; interpolate at runtime, nanosecond-scale overhead, fully deterministic, syncable online.
- Use Dr. Dave's quantitative anchors (3 sidespin levels = 1/2/3 diamonds, etc.) + self-shot 240fps data to apply a residual correction to the table; the >2.5m/s domain must be extrapolated with real footage data (the rigid-body model fails there).
- Also look up v1's shader deformation amplitude/duration from the table (impulse→deformation), so the visuals and physics are self-consistent.

**v3 (real soft body: not done by default; two exceptions)**
- It is **not recommended** to use runtime FEM/XPBD as authoritative gameplay physics: calibration data is missing, determinism/online/replay are difficult, and professional players judge the outgoing mapping rather than the solver's identity; academia does not have even one FEM paper on billiard cushions, so you would be the first to hit the pitfalls.
- Exception A (visual luxury): a local XPBD strip (Small Steps substeps) as purely visual soft body, with the trajectory still decided by the v2 lookup table—low risk, high visual payoff.
- Exception B (data factory): if real footage is constrained, a one-off offline FEM (Abaqus/LS-Dyna, Ogden+Prony, the material parameters require testing your own K-66 specimen strips) can generate lookup-table data for the >2.5m/s domain and deformation animation assets; but first evaluate whether "buy a Chronos and shoot for a day" is cheaper—in most cases it is.
