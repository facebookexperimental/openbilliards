\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The literature behind this paper spans nearly two centuries, and it
organizes naturally into five threads: the analytic foundations of billiard
mechanics; the single-model validation era whose best practice we
generalize; the event-driven simulators and computational players whose
formulation we inherit; the learning-era systems that consume billiards
physics as ground truth; and the verification, validation, and
reproducibility standards that this domain has so far left unapplied.

\subsection{Foundations, 1835--1995}

The analytic mechanics of billiards begins with Coriolis's 1835 treatise
\citep{coriolis1835}, which derived, among other results, the parabolic
path of a sliding cue ball after impact, the aiming construction for the
mass\'e shot, and the invariance of the friction direction during impact.
The treatise remained difficult to obtain for most of its history;
Nadler's annotated English translation \citep{nadler2005coriolis} restored
it to working accessibility. These results are not merely ancestral here:
experiments \expid{D1}--\expid{D3} re-derive the parabola, the mass\'e
construction, and the swerve behavior event-exactly and measure where each
idealization degrades (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Fig.~\ref{fig:F10}).

Controlled measurement arrived more than a century later, one rule at a
time. \citet{bayes1963collision} measured the speed loss in billiard-ball
collisions; \citet{wallace1988billiard} analyzed billiard-ball collisions
in two dimensions, and \citet{onoda1989comment} corrected their treatment
of post-collision rolling. These short papers established the genre this
study generalizes --- and they also exhibit its archetypal limitation:
each supplies a validation point without a validity domain.
\citet{marlow1995physics} closed the era with the first book-length
synthesis to attempt parameterized realism across the whole game; several
of its catalog constants were later superseded by measured curves, a
history reflected in the anchor-tier discipline of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, which ranks published measured curves above
catalog constants.

\subsection{Single-model validation era}

Between the late 1990s and the mid-2010s, individual sub-models of
cue-sports mechanics acquired rigorous derivations and, in the best cases,
instrumented validation. Shepard's monographs
\citep{shepard1997amateur,shepard2001squirt} bridged playing lore to
rigid-body mechanics, including a squirt formalization that precedes the
endmass theory of \citet{cross2008squirt}; Alciatore's technical-proof
corpus \citep{alciatore2008coriolis,alciatore_tp_catalog} maintains the
modern statements of the empirical laws together with measured curves and
is the largest single anchor source for the battery of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. \citet{cohen2002threecushion} formalized
the corner-5 diamond system, but geometrically, without dynamics.
\citet{han2005dynamics} introduced the parameterized impulse model of
cushion impact for carom billiards that our kernel ships, in corrected
form, as \code{han2005_default} (Section~\ref{sec:simulator});
\citet{domenech2008nonsmooth} developed non-smooth two-stage models of
ball collisions; and \citet{cross2008squirt} derived the endmass theory of
cue--ball deflection that anchors the closed form of experiment \expid{B1}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:F6}). Analytic treatments of single-shot mechanics continue
to appear \citep{kim2022motions,kim2024collision}.

The strongest validation practice of this era is that of Mathavan,
Jackson, and Parkin: high-speed imaging to measure billiards dynamics
\citep{mathavan2009highspeed}, a cushion-impact theory validated against
that footage \citep{mathavan2010cushion}, and numerical simulation of
frictional ball--ball collisions on cloth \citep{mathavan2014numerical}.
Instrumented measurement, quantified comparison error, published parameter
sets --- this is the discipline our methodology adopts per anchor. It
remained, however, validation of single sub-models against the authors'
own footage; to our knowledge, no whole-engine, multi-source battery ---
much less a systematic domains-of-validity study --- followed from it.
Experiment \expid{C4} overlays our rebound-coupling predictions directly
on the measured curves of \citet[Figs.~7--10]{mathavan2010cushion}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:F9}), and Section~\ref{sec:simulator} records where our
pool-specification cushion geometry deliberately departs from their
snooker rail profile --- a domain separation, not a disagreement.

A parallel thread in multibody impact mechanics feeds the break resolver
of contribution C3. Continuous contact-force models with hysteresis
damping originate with Lankarani and Nikravesh
\citep{lankarani1990contact,lankarani1994continuous}, and Stronge's
energetic coefficient of restitution \citep{stronge2018impact} supplies
the consistency condition our resolver enforces. Applied to pool,
\citet{corral2020poolballs} modeled pool-ball interaction with these
tools, and \citet{gismerosmoreno2022multiple} extended the approach to
multiple simultaneous impacts --- the closest prior art to C3. The
difference is architectural: they integrate the entire scene under a
smooth penalty formulation, whereas we confine compliant integration to
the frozen-cluster episode inside an otherwise closed-form event stream
(Section~\ref{sec:simulator}). \citet{cross2023oblique} treats small
frozen clusters analytically, delimiting what conservation laws alone can
determine --- precisely the degeneracy that makes a compliant resolver
necessary at break scale.

\subsection{Event-driven simulators and computational players}

\citet{leckie2006event} formulated pool physics as event-driven
simulation: closed-form ball states between events, with event times
obtained as real roots of polynomials of degree at most four. Our kernel
descends from this formulation. Leckie and Greenspan also flagged its
central numerical hazard --- the fragility of polynomial root finding near
grazing configurations; we address it with the quartic solver of
\citet{orellana2020algorithm1010}, incorporating both published
corrections \citep{demichele2022remark,demichele2024remark}, together with
the first-crossing branch selection discussed in Section~\ref{sec:oracle}
(contribution C5).

That formulation was built to serve computational players, and a
productive lineage followed: the PickPocket search-based player
\citep{smith2007pickpocket}, the Deep Green pool-playing robot
\citep{greenspan2008toward}, and the computational-pool tournaments with
their winning entries \citep{archibald2009analysis,%
archibald2010computational,archibald2016distributed};
robotics-assistive systems continue the line \citep{park2022carom}.
Throughout this lineage the physics substrate is instrumental --- a
planning model --- and was never independently validated against physical
measurement; reported evaluation concerns the agent's tournament
performance rather than the simulator's comparison error.

The closest contemporary system is pooltool \citep{kiefl2024pooltool}, an
open-source event-driven billiards simulator distributed as a Python
package. Our relationship to pooltool is cooperative: it serves as the
cross-simulator oracle of our battery, and where the two model registries
coincide we quantify agreement event-by-event (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}).
Where the simulators deliberately differ --- break-cluster resolution,
cushion geometry, and the velocity-dependent cushion restitution
$e_c(v)$ --- each departure is stated with its evidence
(Sections~\ref{sec:simulator} and~\ref{sec:oracle}). The two codebases
also share a reference-implementation lineage, and the case study of
Section~\ref{sec:oracle} shows the battery detecting a slip-impulse
convention inherited through exactly that shared ancestry (the slip-law
defect) --- an argument for independent
cross-validation even between closely related simulators.

\subsection{Learning-era consumers}

Billiards has meanwhile become a standard vehicle for machine-learning
research on physical reasoning. \citet{fragkiadaki2016learning} trained
visual predictive models to play simulated billiards; DeepPHY
\citep{xu2025deepphy} benchmarks agentic vision--language models on
physical-reasoning tasks that include billiards; and CueTip
\citep{memery2025cuetip} builds an interactive, physics-aware pool
assistant on a simulator backend. The common pattern across all three is
that the simulator is trusted as ground truth: whatever model error it
carries transfers, unquantified, into trained policies and benchmark
conclusions. Contributions C1 and C2 make that transferred error
measurable for the cue-sports domain, and the reinforcement-learning
program this motivates is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{The reproducibility gap}

The standards needed to close the remaining gap exist; they have simply
not been applied in this domain. ASME V\&V~20 \citep{asme2009vv20}
defines the vocabulary --- validation variables and points, comparison
error $E = S - D$, validation uncertainty $u_{\mathrm{val}}$ --- that
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} adopts while keeping verification and
validation strictly distinct; the FAIR principles \citep{wilkinson2016fair}
and their research-software extension FAIR4RS \citep{chuehong2022fair4rs}
define the artifact posture. To our knowledge, however, no prior
cue-sports simulator reports bit-exact expected outputs, a
cross-platform determinism audit, or a machine-readable deviation ledger.
The substrate that makes bit-exactness practical is WebAssembly, whose
execution semantics are deterministic up to an enumerated set of escapes
\citep{wasm2015nondeterminism}, each excluded by the kernel by
construction (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}). This paper accordingly imports the
V\&V~20/FAIR toolkit into cue-sports simulation and adds the artifact
class the toolkit lacks: the hash-identified canonical binary as the
physics definition of record (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).
